How the peg pyramid works

The board is a staggered triangle of pegs. Each row is wider than the one above it, so the ball keeps making left-or-right decisions as it falls. By the time it reaches the bottom, it has crossed enough rows to land in one of several pockets, with one more pocket than there are rows.

That repeated 50/50 motion creates a predictable pattern over time: most balls cluster near the center, while edge landings are less common. Statistically, it behaves like a Galton board and follows a binomial curve. In practical terms, the middle pockets tend to pay less often and the outer pockets pay more when they do land.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is built around the odds of landing there, with a small house margin included. When the probability of a pocket and its payout are multiplied together, the result sits a little below a fair 1:1 value. That shortfall is the operator margin, which is why published RTP figures usually sit in the high-90% range.

A simple rule: no row count or risk level changes the long-term math in your favour. The settings change variance, meaning how wild the swings are, not the final average return.
