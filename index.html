How the peg pyramid works

The board is built as a triangle of staggered pegs. Each row is one peg wider than the row above, so a ball that starts at the top has repeated left-or-right decisions as it moves down. By the time it reaches the base, it lands in one of the bottom pockets, with one more pocket than there are rows.

Because every bounce is effectively a coin flip, most balls collect near the center of the board and only a few reach the far edges. Statistically, that shape is known as a Galton board, and the result follows a binomial curve. In Plinko, the payout table simply turns that curve into prize values: common middle pockets pay less, while rare outer pockets pay more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is set in line with its probability, then trimmed slightly to leave a house margin. When the payout of every pocket is weighted by its chance of landing, the total comes in a little under one. That gap is the operator's edge. In most published versions, the return to player sits roughly between 97% and 99%, depending on the studio and the board settings.

A simple rule helps: rows and risk do not improve the average result for the player. They only change how the wins and losses are distributed, which is why the session can feel calmer or sharper without changing the long-run math.

Licensed versions use a random number generator to decide each bounce, and provably fair releases publish seed data so the result can be checked later. What they do not provide is any way to steer the ball by timing a click or using a pattern.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each bounce is determined by an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you verify the round afterward.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add more pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes become larger while their chance gets smaller. The overall return stays nearly the same.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

There is no mathematically better option. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value into rare edge hits. Choose based on how much swing you can handle.
